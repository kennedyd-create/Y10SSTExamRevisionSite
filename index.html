Command words

Underline the command word before you write. It tells you what kind of answer gets the marks.

Name / DefineShort, precise facts or a definition. No explanation needed.
DescribeWhat it looks like or what happened. Who, what, where, when, with numbers.
ExplainWhy or how. Use “because”, “this leads to”, “as a result”.
Compare / EvaluateSimilarities and differences, or a judgement backed by evidence.

In the exam

  • Marks = points. A 3-mark question needs three clear points.
  • Use names, places, dates and numbers as evidence, and real NZ examples where you can.
  • Never leave a blank. A sensible attempt can still earn marks.
  • Save time for the 25-mark History essay.
  • Check spelling, capital letters, units and macrons (Māori, Taupō, Aotearoa).

G5

Natural hazards

A natural hazard is a natural event that threatens people and property. It becomes a natural disaster when it causes major loss of life or damage.

TectonicClimatic
Caused by movement of the Earth's crust: earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, tsunamisCaused by weather and climate: cyclones, floods, droughts, storms

NZ's tectonic setting: NZ sits on the boundary of the Pacific and Australian plates. In the North Island the Pacific Plate subducts under the Australian Plate, causing volcanoes. In the South Island the plates slide past each other along the Alpine Fault.

Vocabulary: tectonic plate, plate boundary, fault, epicentre, focus, magnitude, seismic waves, subduction, magma, lava, lahar, liquefaction, aftershock, low pressure, storm surge, evacuation, state of emergency, mitigation.

G7

Interpreting cartoons

A cartoon makes a point about an issue, usually by poking fun at someone. Work through it in this order.

  1. Look: Who are the characters? What is the setting? Read every label, sign and speech bubble.
  2. Symbols: What do objects stand for? A sinking island can stand for rising sea levels; a suit and tie usually means a politician or businessperson.
  3. Exaggeration: What has been made bigger or more extreme than in real life? That is what the cartoonist wants you to notice.
  4. Issue: What real-world topic is it about?
  5. Message: What is the cartoonist criticising, and whose perspective do they take?
  6. Irony: Look for where what happens is the opposite of what you'd expect, or where a character can't see something obvious.

Sentence starters: “The cartoon shows… The ___ symbolises… The cartoonist is criticising… This is ironic because…”

H5

The essay: plan it with PEEL

Aim for 300–400 words: an introduction, three body paragraphs and a conclusion.

  1. Introduction: name the movement and event, where and when, and what you will argue about its impact.
  2. Paragraph 1 – Background: why did it happen? What was the problem?
  3. Paragraph 2 – The event: what happened and who led it, with names, dates, numbers and quotes.

PEEL in action

PointEvidenceExplainLink

The bombing of the Rainbow Warrior turned many New Zealanders firmly against nuclear weapons. On 10 July 1985, French secret agents set off two bombs on the Greenpeace ship in Auckland's Waitematā Harbour, killing photographer Fernando Pereira. Because a foreign government had carried out a deadly attack on NZ soil against peaceful protesters, public anger grew and support for the anti-nuclear movement increased. As a result, the bombing strengthened New Zealand’s nuclear-free stance, which became law in 1987.

E3

Demand and supply

Law of Demand: as price rises, quantity demanded falls. The demand curve slopes down from left to right.

Law of Supply: as price rises, quantity supplied rises, because higher prices mean more profit. The supply curve slopes up.

Equilibrium: where D and S cross; quantity demanded = quantity supplied. Price too high → surplus. Price too low → shortage.

Graph checklist: title · Price ($) on the y-axis · Quantity on the x-axis · even scale · curves labelled D and S · equilibrium price (Pe) and quantity (Qe) marked with dotted lines.

Shifts demandShifts supply
Income · tastes and fashion · advertising · price of substitutes (Coke vs Pepsi) · price of complements (phones and cases) · population · season or weatherCosts of production (wages, materials) · technology · productivity · weather (farming) · number of producers · taxes and subsidies · price of related goods

Increase = curve shifts right. Decrease = curve shifts left. A change in the good's own price moves along the curve; it doesn't shift it.

E4

Imports, exports and the balance of trade

Imports: coming inExports: going out
Goods and services bought from overseas. Money leaves NZ.Goods and services sold to other countries. Money enters NZ.
Vehicles, machinery and technology, fuel, electronics, clothing and textilesDairy, meat, wood and logs, fruit (kiwifruit, apples), wine, and tourism (a service that brings money in)

Why trade? Countries don't have all the resources they need, because geography and climate limit what they can make. Early iwi traded food such as dried fish and kūmara, pounamu, tools and materials with other iwi and Pacific peoples for the same reason. Nā tō rourou, nā taku rourou ka ora ai te iwi: with your food basket and my food basket the people will thrive.

Trading partners: China is our largest partner (huge demand for dairy). Australia is close and has free trade agreements with us. The USA and Japan are major markets for meat and technology. Trade routes are shaped by distance, colonial history and demand for resources. As an island nation, most of our trade goes by sea, from ports like Tauranga and Auckland to ports like Shanghai and Singapore.

Balance of trade = value of exports − value of imports. Exports bigger → trade surplus. Imports bigger → trade deficit. E.g. exports $70bn − imports $75bn = a $5bn deficit.

Why the imbalance? China is a manufacturing powerhouse (secondary sector), so it has a big surplus. NZ relies on raw materials (primary sector) and imports high-value machinery and electronics, so it often runs a deficit.

Impacts of a trade deficit

  • More money leaves NZ than comes in.
  • NZ businesses may lose sales if people buy cheaper imports instead of local goods, e.g. imported wool jumpers.
  • The NZ dollar may weaken, because more NZ dollars are exchanged for foreign currency to buy imports. This makes imports even dearer.

E5

Specialisation, comparative advantage and adding value

SpecialisationFocusing on producing the good or service you are best at, instead of trying to make everything. You produce more, and better quality. A doctor doesn't bake their own bread.
Comparative advantageBeing able to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than others. It's about opportunity cost, not just who is best.
Absolute advantageBeing able to produce more of something, or produce it faster, than someone else.
EfficiencyGetting more output from the same amount of time or resources.
Adding valueTurning a raw material into a finished product that sells for a higher price, e.g. milk → cheese, logs → furniture, grapes → bottled Waiheke wine.
Living standardsThe level of wealth, comfort, material goods and necessities available to people.

Fish and coconuts: you take 60 minutes to catch a fish and 30 minutes to pick a coconut, so 1 fish costs you 2 coconuts. Friday takes 20 minutes per fish and 30 per coconut, so 1 fish costs him only 0.67 coconuts. Friday has the comparative advantage in fishing. If you pick coconuts and he fishes, then you trade, you both end up with more food for the same work.

Why countries specialise: climate (NZ's rain grows grass for cows), natural resources (Saudi Arabia has oil; NZ has iron sand and fish), skills (generations of farming knowledge) and technology (dairy robotics). NZ specialises in dairy and wool, wine and horticulture (Marlborough), and tourism. NZ specialises in dairy while Australia specialises in minerals, because each has a lower opportunity cost.

Small countries gain the most: NZ has limited land and workers and can't make everything efficiently, so it focuses on a few things, produces them at large scale, and trades for the rest.

Why adding value matters: it earns more income (higher selling price), creates more local jobs (factory workers, winemakers, designers) and keeps more profit in the NZ economy, which raises living standards.

E6

Exchange rates

Exchange rateThe value of one currency in terms of another, e.g. NZ$1 = US$0.60. Think of it as the “price” of money. We need it for travel, online shopping and trade.
AppreciationThe NZD gets stronger and buys more foreign currency, e.g. US$0.60 → US$0.70. It has more purchasing power.
DepreciationThe NZD gets weaker and buys less, e.g. US$0.70 → US$0.60. Shopping on US websites costs more.
Purchasing powerHow much your money can buy.

Converting to NZ dollars: when the rate is written as NZ$1 = x foreign currency, divide the foreign price by the rate. A$8.50 ÷ 0.92 = NZ$9.24. Round money to 2 decimal places.

NZD depreciates (weaker)NZD appreciates (stronger)
Winners: exporters (dairy, kiwifruit) and tourism, because our goods look cheap overseas

Losers: exporters and tourism operators, because NZ looks expensive to overseas buyers

A weak dollar isn't always bad: it helps our exporters sell more. The NZ dollar is also used in the Cook Islands, Niue, Tokelau and the Pitcairn Islands.

Write it

Is trade good for New Zealand? (6 marks)

One benefit, one negative, then your judgement. Use NZ examples.

Show a model answer
One benefit of trade for New Zealand is the income it brings in. For example, dairy is our biggest export, and selling milk powder to China earns billions of dollars, which creates jobs for farmers, truck drivers and port workers and gives the government tax money for hospitals and schools.

However, one negative is that cheap imports can put local businesses out of work. Imported clothing is often cheaper than NZ-made clothing, so some NZ factories have closed and workers have lost their jobs. Trade also has environmental costs, because producing more dairy for export leads to water pollution and methane emissions.

Overall, I think that trade is good for New Zealand, because as a small country we cannot make everything ourselves, but we need to manage its effects on local jobs and the environment.
E8

Global inequality and development

InequalityWhen some people or countries have much greater access to money, resources, education, healthcare and opportunities than others.
Economic developmentImproving a country's economy and people's standard of living over time.
Standard of livingThe level of wealth, comfort and goods available to people.
GDP per capitaTotal value of everything a country produces divided by its population. Measures income only.
HDIHuman Development Index: combines life expectancy (health), education and income. Shows quality of life better than GDP alone.
Brandt LineA line drawn in 1980 that roughly divides the richer Global North from the poorer Global South. It loops around Australia and NZ to include them in the North. Some countries no longer fit it, e.g. Singapore and South Korea are now wealthy.

Causes of inequality: history (some countries were stripped of resources during colonisation), geography (landlocked countries struggle to trade because they have no ports; many of the world's poorest countries are landlocked), stability (war and corruption stop long-term investment), and access to education and natural resources.

Impacts of inequality: unequal access to education, healthcare and sanitation; lower quality of life; tension between the “haves” and “have nots”; social problems and unrest.

E9

Evaluating foreign aid

Foreign aidMoney or material help that wealthy countries give developing countries to support economic growth and social welfare.
ODAOfficial Development Assistance: the government budget set aside for foreign aid to developing countries.
Bilateral aidCountry → country. Direct from one government to another, e.g. NZ gives money to Fiji's government to build a hospital.
Multilateral aidCountry → organisation → countries. Given to groups like the UN or World Bank, which share it out, e.g. NZ funds UNICEF, which runs vaccinations in many countries.
Emergency aidShort-term help after a disaster: food, water, shelter, medicine.
Capacity buildingLong-term help that builds skills and systems: schools, training, clean water, roads.
Aid dependencyWhen a country relies so heavily on aid that it struggles to grow its own economy. Local industries can't develop, and the country stays dependent on donors' decisions.
FDIForeign Direct Investment: an overseas business invests in a country, e.g. builds a factory. Creates lasting jobs.

Foreign aid quiz

Compare short-term disaster relief with long-term infrastructure aid. Which is more beneficial for a developing nation in the long run, and why?

Use Point, Explain, Evidence, Link.

Show a model answer
Short-term disaster relief, such as the food, water and tents NZ sends after a Pacific cyclone, is essential because it saves lives straight away. However, it does not change the country's situation once the emergency is over. In contrast, long-term infrastructure aid, such as building schools, roads or a hospital in Fiji, builds the country's capacity to support itself. Better health and education raise HDI and help people find work, so the country becomes less likely to need aid in the future. Therefore I believe long-term infrastructure aid is more beneficial in the long run, because it reduces the risk of aid dependency, although emergency aid is still needed when disasters strike.
